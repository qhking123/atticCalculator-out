Faced vs Unfaced Batt Insulation — Vapor Barrier Rules by Climate Zone

The kraft paper on a faced batt is a vapor retarder, and its direction is decided by physics, not preference: warm side in winter, always. Get the climate zone wrong and the same paper that protects a Minnesota attic grows mold in a Georgia one. This guide turns the whole decision into a zone matrix, an install SOP, and a pre-flight checklist.

Applies to

Any fiberglass batt job — attic floors, rafter bays, walls

Time needed

2–4 hours for a 1000 sq ft attic floor (DIY pace)

Difficulty

Beginner-friendly — the physics does the hard part

What Is Faced vs Unfaced Insulation?

Both products are the same fiberglass — R-3.2–4.0 per inch, same bags on the shelf, same thermal math. The entire difference is one layer on one side, and that layer changes where each product belongs.

Faced batt = fiberglass + kraft paper

A kraft-paper backing is laminated to one side, doing two jobs at once: it serves as a Class II vapor retarder (0.5–1.0 perm) that slows moisture diffusion into the cavity, and its protruding tabs form the stapling flange that fixes the batt to joists or studs.

Perm rating: 0.5–1.0 (Class II retarder)

Direction: kraft face toward the warm side

Install: staples through the paper tabs

Cost: ~5–10% more than unfaced

Unfaced batt = bare fiberglass

No backing, no flange, no vapor control — thermal resistance only. Where a barrier is required, it comes separately as a 6-mil polyethylene sheet (Class I, ≤0.1 perm) stapled to the warm side before the batts go in.

Perm rating: essentially open (no facing)

Direction: n/a — add poly if the zone needs it

Install: friction-fit or wire supports

Cost: cheapest batt option

💡 Which is "better"? Neither — the climate zone picks. Faced wins in Zones 5–7 where winter vapor drive needs blocking; unfaced wins in Zones 1–4 where assemblies need to dry. Why Vapor Barrier Direction Matters More Than Anything Else

Moisture moves from warm to cold. In a heating climate, warm humid indoor air diffuses outward toward the cold sheathing; if it reaches a cold surface inside the cavity, it condenses to liquid. The vapor barrier's only job is to stop that diffusion before the air reaches a surface cold enough to condense on — which is why it lives on the warm side of the insulation.

Correct — warm side

Living space — warm & humid (22°C)
🛡️ Vapor barrier — HERE
Insulation (warm, stays dry)
Sheathing — cold (16°C)

Moisture never reaches the cold surface. The cavity stays dry all winter.

Wrong — cold side

Living space — warm & humid (22°C)
Insulation (moisture passes through)
🛡️ Vapor barrier — WRONG SIDE
Sheathing — cold (16°C)

Warm air diffuses through the batt, condenses on the barrier's cold face — trapped wet insulation, mold, lost R-value.

The failure is seasonal and cumulative: nothing looks wrong in October, the staining shows up in February, and the mold claim arrives two winters later. This is why the checklist below puts “identify the warm side” before “open the bags.” In cooling-dominated climates the drive reverses — which is exactly why Zones 1–3 skip the barrier entirely rather than flip it.

Climate Zone Decision Matrix — Faced or Unfaced, Zone by Zone

The homepage carries the three-row quick rule; this is the full decision tree. Find your zone on the DOE map (or the calculator’s zone selector), then read across. The rule of thumb compresses to one sentence: hot zones go bare, cold zones get a barrier on the warm side.

⚠️ The reversal zones: Mixed climates (4A, and 5 during summer) see vapor drive both directions in a year. This is where smart retarders earn their premium — permeance that rises when the cavity needs to dry.

❌ Don't: Buy faced batts for a Florida attic because they were $6 cheaper on the pallet. Six summers later the cavity behind that kraft paper is the mold claim nobody wants.

The Fatal Mistakes Ranking — What Causes the Mold

Vapor problems are rare when the assembly is simple and frequent when it is doubled. Ranked by how often each one produces an actual moisture claim, not by how obvious it looks on paper.

#1

Two vapor barriers stacked

MOST COMMON

6-mil poly stapled over faced batts — or new faced batts over old faced batts. Moisture that gets between the two layers can dry to neither side. This is the most common mold mechanism in retrofitted cavities: the existing barrier was fine; adding a second created the trap.

#2

Barrier installed on the cold side

Poly or kraft facing pointing away from the living space. Winter diffusion condenses on the barrier's cold face, wetting the insulation from the outside in. Common in DIY jobs that copy a photo instead of reading the zone.

#3

Faced batts in Zones 1–3

Kraft paper on the interior of a hot-humid wall traps summer's inward-driven moisture. The paper was only $6 cheaper — the remediators charge four figures.

#4

Unfaced batts in Zones 5–7 with no poly

The inverse error: a Minnesota wall filled with bare fiberglass and no warm-side retarder quietly condenses all winter. Often invisible until the sheathing is opened during a renovation.

⚠️ Stronger is not safer: A Class I barrier where code wants Class II is a defect, not an upgrade. Over-tight assemblies cannot dry from the warm side, so every incidental leak — a nail hole, a plumbing joint — stays trapped. Match the class to the zone, don't exceed it.

Attic Vapor Barrier vs Wall Vapor Barrier — Different Games

Walls and attics fail differently, so their vapor strategy differs. In a wall cavity, moisture is trapped between sheathing and drywall with no airflow to rescue it — that is where vapor control is most critical and where the zone matrix above applies literally.

A vented attic floor is the opposite: continuous soffit-to-ridge airflow constantly dries the assembly, which is why most modern codes require no attic vapor barrier at all. What the attic needs instead is air sealing — the convective leaks through penetrations move orders of magnitude more moisture than diffusion does. A gap around a bath fan exhaust dumps more water vapor into an attic in one winter than diffusion brings in ten years.

Wall cavities (2×4, 2×6)

Sealed on both sides → vapor barrier decision is critical → follow the zone matrix exactly → kraft faces warm side or poly goes inside.

Vented attic floors

Ventilated above, open below → air sealing beats vapor barrier → blown-in loose-fill or unfaced batts → barrier generally not required.

💡 Priority order for attics: air seal → ventilate → insulate. The homepage’s vapor-barrier rules by climate zone carry the same three-row quick version of the zone table above, and the dedicated baffles & ventilation guide walks the airflow install that dries the deck.

Rafter Bays vs Flat Attic Floor — Where the Paper Goes

The same house can legitimately need two different answers depending on where the batts land. For sloped rafter bays above an insulated ceiling — the triangle between rafters — the assembly behaves like a wall: the vapor barrier sits on the warm side, which is the underside of the ceiling joists, i.e., facing the room below. Faced batts with kraft toward the floor (or poly on the ceiling side) are correct there.

For a flat attic floor under a vented roof, the open deck changes everything: blown-in loose-fill covers joists and cavities uniformly, there are no enclosed cavities to trap moisture, and ventilation dries the deck from above. Unfaced anything works; most retrofit work is blown cellulose precisely because it leaves no facing decision to get wrong.

⚠️ The conversion trap: Finishing the attic later — closing rafter bays into conditioned rooms — flips the assembly from “ventilated floor” to “sealed cavity.” The vapor rules then apply to the new roof deck, and the vented vs unvented attic decision determines whether foam or batts+vents is the right assembly. Decide that before the drywall, not after.

Faced Batt Install SOP — Seven Steps, With Times

A 1000 sq ft attic floor takes two people 2–4 hours at DIY pace. The steps are ordered; step 1 exists because it undoes the other six when skipped.

1

Inspect for existing vapor barriers

Critical step⏱ 15 min

Look for old poly sheeting, kraft paper on existing batts, or foil facing before anything new arrives. Found one? The new layer must be unfaced — this single check prevents the #1 fatal mistake.

2

Confirm zone & batt choice

⏱ 10 min

Match the matrix above: 1–4 unfaced, 5–8 faced (or unfaced + poly). Cross-check the county amendment.

3

Install soffit baffles first

⏱ 45–60 min

Every eave bay gets a baffle before batts or blow obstructs the soffit vents. Staple to the rafter at the eave, keep the air channel open.

4

Cut with the paper up — paper first, fiberglass second

⏱ 20 min per batch

Lay the batt kraft-side down on a plywood sheet, compress with a straightedge, score the paper with a utility knife, then slice through the fibers. Cutting fiberglass-first shreds the edge and the flange.

5

Staple the flanges — every 6–8 inches

Critical step⏱ 2–3 min per bay

Face-staple (flange flat on the joist) for vapor continuity, or inset-staple (flange inside the bay) for drywall contact — pick one method and stay consistent. Kraft faces the warm side, always.

6

Seam overlap 6 inches + tape

⏱ 15 min

Where unfaced work includes a poly layer, overlap seams 6 inches and seal with proper vapor-barrier tape. Butt kraft flanges snugly — no tape needed between faced batts, but no gaps either.

7

Verify — then tape the staple holes

Verification⏱ 10 min

Walk the whole job: warm side correct, no double barriers, no compressed edges, baffles visible at every eave. Low-tack tape over every staple tear — each puncture is a pinhole vapor leak.

🧣Flanges stapled, kraft facing the warm side
Flanges stapled, kraft facing the warm sidePhoto: Unsplash

❌ Strictly forbidden: Stapling through the fiberglass face “to hold it tight” — every staple through glass fiber tears the flange and leaves the paper loose. Staples go through paper only.

⚠️ Rollback rule: If step 1 finds poly you did not expect, stop and re-plan — proceeding turns a 15-minute discovery into a 4-figure remediation. If any step stalls more than 30 minutes, close the bags and call a pro rather than improvise the vapor layer.

Code Differences by Region — When the Map Isn't Enough

The IECC zone map is the baseline; counties amend it. A few patterns are stable enough to expect: Minnesota and New York enforce warm-side vapor control in cold zones — expect a 6-mil poly or equivalent Class II requirement on new insulated assemblies. California runs its own Title 24 climate zones, and some coastal-zone amendments still ask for an interior retarder even where the national map says optional.

Hot-humid states — Florida and the Gulf Coast — sit at the opposite extreme: their codes increasingly discourage interior-side barriers outright, because two generations of trapped-summer-moisture mold claims taught the building departments a lesson. Some jurisdictions along the humid Southeast go further and specify smart retarders for mixed seasonal drives.

The practical move costs one phone call: ask your local building department for the current IECC supplement and the vapor-retarder requirement for your assembly type. Inspectors enforce the supplement, not the national map — and the supplier will happily sell you either product without asking where you live. This check is box 2 on the checklist for a reason.

Spray Foam Changes Everything — and Ends the Debate

Closed-cell spray foam collapses this entire guide into one product: it is simultaneously the insulation, the air barrier, and the vapor barrier (Class II or better at 2+ inches). A closed-cell roof deck needs no poly, no kraft facing, and no zone-table gymnastics — which is exactly why unvented conditioned attics are built with it. The trade is price: $1.50–$3.00 per square foot per inch, professional-only application.

Open-cell spray foam is different — soft, vapor-open, and it needs help. At R-3.5–4.5 per inch it insulates and air-seals, but moisture diffuses through it, so assemblies using open-cell on a roof deck typically add a warm-side vapor retarder (often a smart membrane or code-approved coating) to keep winter diffusion out of the sheathing.

❌ Don't: Add poly over closed-cell foam — you are stacking a Class I barrier over another vapor barrier, recreating mistake #1 at premium prices. Foam assemblies manage their own vapor; let them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is faced insulation?▼

Faced insulation is a fiberglass batt with a kraft-paper facing laminated to one side. The facing does two jobs at once: it acts as a Class II vapor retarder (perm rating 0.5–1.0), slowing moisture diffusion into the cavity, and it forms a stapling flange — the paper tabs on each edge that fasten the batt to the joists or studs.

What is unfaced insulation?▼

Unfaced insulation is a bare fiberglass batt — no kraft paper, no foil, no flange. It supplies thermal resistance only, so any vapor control has to come from a separate layer such as a 6-mil polyethylene sheet on the warm side, or from nothing at all in hot-humid climates where a vapor barrier is deliberately omitted.

Is faced insulation a vapor barrier?▼

Technically it is a vapor retarder, not a full barrier. Kraft paper is Class II — permeance between 0.5 and 1.0 perm — which slows moisture diffusion substantially but does not stop it the way Class I materials do (6-mil poly at ≤0.1 perm). For most attic-floor and wall applications, Class II control is exactly what codes want; a true Class I barrier is reserved for very cold climates or high-humidity rooms.

What is the difference between a vapor retarder and a vapor barrier?▼

The difference is perm rating. Class I vapor barriers (poly sheet, foil) measure 0.1 perm or less — nearly impermeable. Class II retarders (kraft paper) measure 0.5–1.0 perm — slow but not sealed. Class III retarders (latex paint) measure 1–10 perms. Building codes specify which class each wall or ceiling assembly needs; using a stronger class than required is a common cause of trapped moisture.

Which side does the vapor barrier go on in an attic?▼

Always the warm-in-winter side — facing the living space below. In most U.S. homes that means the kraft face points down toward the ceiling drywall, or the poly sheet staples to the underside of the ceiling joists before insulation goes in. Putting the barrier on the cold (outdoor) side traps indoor moisture inside the cavity where it condenses.

Do I need a vapor barrier on my attic floor?▼

Usually not. Modern codes generally do not require a vapor barrier on vented attic floors because soffit-to-ridge ventilation plus air sealing handles moisture control — and blown-in loose-fill covers the whole deck, so there is no batt facing to worry about. The vapor decisions matter in framed cavities: rafter bays and walls, not open attic floors.

Can I use faced batts in a hot-humid climate?▼

You can, but you shouldn't in most assemblies. In Zones 1–3 (Florida, Gulf Coast, humid Southeast), the moisture drive reverses in summer — hot humid air pushes inward, and a kraft face on the wrong side traps that moisture against the cavity. Best practice is unfaced batts with no vapor barrier, letting the assembly dry inward and outward as the season demands.

What is 6-mil poly sheeting and when do I need it?▼

A 6-mil polyethylene sheet is a Class I vapor barrier (≤0.1 perm) stapled to the warm side of a cavity. Cold climates (Zones 5–7) that insulate with unfaced batts typically require it — Minnesota and New York among them. It must overlap 6 inches at seams, get sealed with vapor-barrier tape, and sit alone: adding it over faced batts creates the double-barrier mold trap.

Can I layer unfaced batts over faced batts?▼

Yes — this is the standard topping-up pattern. The existing faced batt keeps its kraft face toward the warm side (it is already there; peeling it out destroys more value than it protects), and the new unfaced layer goes over it without adding a second barrier. Never lay a new faced layer's paper over old paper, and never add poly over faced batts.

What happens if I install the vapor barrier backwards?▼

In winter, warm indoor air carries moisture into the cavity, hits the cold poly or kraft face on the wrong (outdoor) side, and condenses there — inside the insulation, against the coldest surface. The result is wet insulation with lost R-value, dark staining, and eventually mold on the sheathing. The fix is surgical: open the assembly, flip or remove the barrier, and let everything dry before closing it up.

Do fiberglass batts need a vapor barrier in Zone 4?▼

In most of Zone 4, no. Mixed-humid 4A and mixed-dry 4B jurisdictions generally accept unfaced batts with no added barrier, following IECC's standard wall assembly. A few local amendments — parts of California and the mountain West — still ask for an interior-side retarder, which is why the checklist says to confirm your county's IECC supplement before buying faced or unfaced.

How do I staple faced batts properly?▼

Two accepted methods: face-stapling puts the paper flange flat on the joist or stud face (better vapor continuity), inset-stapling tucks the flange inside the cavity (better drywall contact). Either way, staple the tabs every 6–8 inches, cut the kraft paper first with a utility knife before cutting the fiberglass, and tape over any staple tears — each puncture is a tiny vapor leak.
