Chapter 01

Why this site exists

An uninsulated or under-insulated attic is the biggest single energy leak in most homes. Heat rises, and in a home without proper attic insulation that heat climbs straight through the ceiling, into the attic, and out through the roof. In winter that means your furnace runs more to replace the heat that escaped. In summer it means your attic heats up to 150°F, baking the ceiling above the second-floor rooms and forcing your air conditioner to work harder to overcome the downward heat load. A properly insulated attic is the single highest-ROI energy upgrade most homeowners can make — and yet the information needed to plan it is scattered across DOE PDFs, manufacturer spec sheets, and blog posts that stop short of the actual bag count.

Look at what most of the internet offers instead. Generic square-footage calculators that ignore roof pitch entirely — they tell you how many square feet of floor you have, not how many square feet of sloped ceiling you actually need to cover. Supplier sizing charts that want to sell you their specific product and quietly assume their R-value is the only one that matters. Forum threads that end in "call a contractor" without telling you what you should ask them to quote. Meanwhile the real decisions — cellulose vs. fiberglass, blown-in vs. batts, DIY vs. professional, Zone 4A vs. Zone 5 — wait on a number nobody wants to calculate twice.

We built AtticCalculator.com to close that gap with the boring, correct version of the trade's own method: zone + geometry + material → R-value gap, bag count, project cost, annual savings & payback period. No one-size-fits-all shortcuts. No mystery constants. The zone presets carry the actual ENERGY STAR recommended R-values for each DOE climate band, the material library carries manufacturer-published R-values per inch, and the cost output shows the labor at $1.5/sq ft dynamic rate instead of a fixed number plucked from thin air. The calculator is the product; this page is the contract — how the engine works, what it promises, and the standards the guides around it answer to.

Chapter 02

From square feet and zone to payback, in five stops

Every input follows the same sequence, and the sequence is public. Because the method never changes, two people checking the same attic can compare numbers line by line — and two answers from this tool are mathematically impossible.

1

Stop 1

Map Your Attic Geometry

Gross floor area in square feet, roof pitch (4/12, 6/12, 10/12 — rise over run), cutouts for chimneys and skylights, and whether you are insulating the floor or the roof slope. The slope factor √(1 + (pitch/12)²) converts your footprint into the actual surface area the insulation must cover — a 6/12 pitch adds 13% area, a 10/12 pitch adds 36%.

2

Stop 2

Pick Your Climate Zone

The U.S. Department of Energy defines eight climate zones. Zone 1 (Miami) targets R-30 attics; Zone 5 (Chicago) pushes R-60. Our calculator maps your zone number to the ENERGY STAR recommended R-value so you never guess. Colder zones demand higher R-values because heat loss through an unconditioned attic is the single biggest winter energy drain.

3

Stop 3

Choose Your Insulation Material

Loose fiberglass, blown cellulose, mineral wool batts, spray foam (open-cell or closed-cell), or rigid XPS/EPS boards — each with its R-value per inch. Cellulose at R-3.2–3.8/inch is the most economical retrofit choice; closed-cell spray foam at R-5–6/inch doubles as a vapor barrier. Pick one preset or layer several in Multi-Layer Mode.

4

Stop 4

Compute the Gap

If your attic already has some insulation, enter the depth and material type. The calculator subtracts your existing R-value from the climate-zone target and tells you exactly how much more you need — or gives you the friendly message that you are already sufficiently insulated and no additional material is required.

5

Stop 5

Get the Bags, Cost & Payback

Coverage per bag or roll at your target depth converts the required R-value gap into an exact shopping list. Cost & ROI Mode adds material price, labor rate ($1.5/sq ft), fuel cost, and annual HVAC spend to produce material cost, labor cost, total project cost, annual savings, 10-year savings, simple payback period in years, and estimated CO₂ reduction.

Climate Zone R-Value Targets

  • DOE / ENERGY STAR Zone 1 (hot-humid): R-30
  • Zones 2–3 (mixed-humid, mixed-dry): R-30/R-38
  • Zone 4A/4B (cold-dry, cold-humid): R-49
  • Zones 5–7 (very cold): R-60
  • Each zone is mapped to the actual ENERGY STAR recommendation, not a rounded guess

30+ Insulation Materials, Layerable

  • Blow-in: loose fiberglass R-2.2–3.2/inch, cellulose R-3.2–3.8/inch, mineral wool R-3.0–3.6/inch
  • Batts/rolls: faced and unfaced fiberglass, mineral wool, cotton batts
  • Foam: open-cell spray R-3.5–4.5/inch, closed-cell R-5.5–6.5/inch, XPS R-5/inch, EPS R-4/inch
  • Air gaps, gypsum, plywood, OSB — the Multi-Layer builder stacks any combination
  • Material presets carry real R-values per inch from manufacturer data sheets

Attic Geometry: Pitch & Cutouts

  • Slope factor auto-computed from rise/run ratio: √(1 + (pitch/12)²)
  • Net area = gross area × slope factor − cutouts (chimneys, skylights, hatches)
  • Joist cavity percentage for batts vs. blown-in calculations
  • Supports both Attic Floor and Roof Slope insulation types

Cost, Savings & Payback

  • Labor dynamically calculated by area ($1.5/sq ft), not a fixed number
  • Fuel cost unit changes with fuel type: $/therm (natural gas), $/gallon (fuel oil), $/kWh (electricity)
  • Annual savings modeled on ENERGY STAR's 15% HVAC reduction base, scaled by R-value added
  • Payback = total cost ÷ annual savings — shown as N/A when current R ≥ target
  • CO₂ reduction estimate attached to every Cost & ROI result

A homeowner counting bags for a weekend project, an HVAC contractor bidding a 50-home retrofit, and an energy auditor scoring a house all want the same numbers — they just read different lines of the result. These are the people we design for.

Homeowners Planning a DIY Job

HVAC & Insulation Contractors

Bid on a project with numbers: square feet after slope adjustment, R-value deficit, material coverage rates, and labor cost per square foot that scales across the job. Turn the calculator into a quote generator by adding material costs and fuel prices on the fly.

Home Energy Raters & Auditors

Score a home, find the attic R-value gap, and present the remediation cost against the projected annual savings — all in one tool. The CO₂ reduction figure lets you tie every attic project to its climate impact, not just its thermal one.

Building Owners & Facility Managers

Large attics in commercial buildings, warehouses, and multi-family properties share the same physics but different scale. The calculator works in both directions: enter a target R and get material count, or enter an existing assembly and get its effective U-value.

Real Estate Agents & Appraisers

When insulation upgrades factor into an energy-efficient home valuation, you need defensible numbers. Show the R-value your attic currently provides, the zone target it should reach, and the upgrade cost — and tie it all to ENERGY STAR recommendations.

Students & Educators

Heat transfer, thermal resistance, building science — every formula on the page, every intermediate value visible. The multi-layer R-value builder is a live lab for understanding how R-values in series add, how air gaps matter, and how U-value is the reciprocal of R.

The common thread

Every audience above needs the same handful of numbers — the R-value gap, the bag count, the project cost, and the annual savings — and needs them defensible. Not "the internet said so," but here is the zone, here is the measured attic area after slope adjustment, here is the material we picked. That defensibility is what turns an attic insulation plan from an opinion into a specification.

Chapter 04

Six decisions an attic R-value calculation feeds

The R-value number is never the deliverable — the bag count, the cost, the payback, and the energy audit are. Here is where the output goes once the engine is done with it.

The Shopping List

The bag or roll count the calculator produces is the number you walk into Home Depot or Lowe's with — no buffer anxiety, no return truckload. Material coverage rates are pulled from manufacturer spec sheets for each product at its rated installed depth.

The Project Budget

Material cost plus dynamic labor cost per square foot gives you the full picture before you call a contractor. Compare DIY (blown-in rental) vs. professional installation by toggling labor to zero and re-adding it with a contractor quote.

The Energy Audit

Home energy audits live on R-values. When your auditor says 'you need R-49,' this calculator tells you how much depth, how many bags, and what it will cost — so you can turn the audit recommendation into an actionable project on the spot.

The Comparison

Cellulose vs. fiberglass vs. spray foam — enter the price per bag and the R-value per inch and watch the total cost-per-R of each option emerge. The calculator removes emotion from the material decision by making every alternative side-by-side comparable.

The Payback Decision

Payback period is the number of years before your energy savings equal your project cost. At $35/bag cellulose in Zone 4A (R-49 target, 1200 sq ft), payback is around 18–24 years. That is the number that answers 'is this worth doing?' — and it changes with fuel prices.

The Code & Compliance Check

Many jurisdictions have energy codes that require minimum attic insulation. The calculator's ENERGY STAR zone targets are designed to meet or exceed most current code requirements, so your project starts compliant and ends documented.

Chapter 05

What we promise — and what we don’t

A calculator earns trust by being precise about its own limits. Here is the complete list of both.

The physics are standard

R_total = Σ(R_per-inch × thickness) for multi-layer assemblies, U = 1/R for thermal transmittance, Q = U × A × ΔT for heat loss — nothing exotic, nothing hidden. The DOE climate zone targets and ENERGY STAR recommendations are applied directly without modification.

Material data from published specs

R-values per inch come from manufacturer product data sheets and recognized industry sources: NAIMA for mineral wool, FPSC for cellulose, ACC for spray foam, and DOE recommendations for fiberglass. If a material updates its published R-value, that number moves in our presets.

Every step visible

A single R-value number hides the engineering. The calculator shows the slope factor, the net area after cutouts, the existing R-value contribution, the target gap, the per-inch R of your material, and the resulting depth — so you can trace every result back to its inputs.

Precision you can reproduce

Slope factors are carried to three decimals. Coverage per bag uses the manufacturer's rated square footage at the target installed depth, not a rounded estimate. Where a material's R-value is a range (loose fiberglass R-2.2–3.2/inch), we let you pick the low or high end instead of averaging — because real material varies.

Estimates, not specifications

Every output is a planning figure. Your actual attic may have more cutouts than you counted, may have existing insulation with settled thickness, may have ventilation issues, and may have a different climate zone than you thought. The calculator starts the conversation; an energy audit or local code official completes it.

FAQ

Straight answers about the site

Tool-specific questions live in the homepage FAQ. These are the ones about us.

How accurate is the attic insulation calculator?
For the inputs you enter, the arithmetic is exact: the slope factor, the net area after cutouts, the gap between existing and target R-value, and the material coverage conversion are deterministic math. The honest uncertainty lives in the inputs — existing insulation depth guessed instead of measured, climate zone assumed instead of verified, cutouts estimated instead of counted, and material R-value picked from a range. Measure the depth in several spots, confirm your zone on the DOE map, and treat the result as a planning-grade estimate that a professional energy audit will refine.
What methodology does the site follow?
The calculator applies U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) climate zone definitions and ENERGY STAR recommended R-values, summed multi-layer thermal resistance R_total = Σ(R_per-inch × thickness), standard slope factor √(1 + (pitch/12)²), and energy savings modeling based on ENERGY STAR's 15% annual HVAC reduction baseline scaled by the fraction of target R-value added. Cost inputs use dynamic labor at $1.5/sq ft and fuel units matched to fuel type.
Where do the material R-values come from?
Presets carry published R-values per inch from recognized industry sources: NAIMA (North American Insulation Manufacturers Association), FPSC (Fire Protection Suppression Council) for cellulose, ACC (American Chemistry Council) for spray foam, and DOE and manufacturer product data sheets for fiberglass and mineral wool. Where a material's R-value is a range (loose fiberglass R-2.2–3.2/inch), the calculator shows the full range and lets you select the low, mid, or high end. Real installed R-values depend on proper depth, uniform coverage, and freedom from compression.
Does the calculator account for roof pitch?
Yes — this is one of the features that distinguishes it from a generic square footage counter. The slope factor formula √(1 + (pitch/12)²) converts your gross attic floor area into the actual surface area of the sloped ceiling. A 4/12 pitch (common in suburban tract homes) adds only 5.5% area; a 6/12 pitch adds 13%; a 10/12 steeply pitched roof adds 36%. The calculator applies this automatically so your bag count reflects the material actually needed, not just the footprint.
Why does the tool warn me not to over-insulate?
Every attic has a ventilation limit. In a properly vented attic, air should flow from soffits to ridge vents to carry away moisture and heat. If you install more insulation than your ventilation can handle — blocking the free air path across the top of the insulation — moisture gets trapped, mold grows, and your R-value silently drops as damp fiberglass or cellulose conducts heat instead of resisting it. The calculator's friendly message when current R ≥ target means you are done; the next step is ventilation, not more material.
Can I use this for walls and floors too?
The calculator is designed for attics specifically — the climate zone targets are attic-specific (walls and floors have different minimums), and the geometry presets (roof slope, cutouts, joist cavities) assume attic applications. However, the Multi-Layer Mode's R-value builder works conceptually for any assembly: enter the layers of a wall section or a floor section and it will sum their R-values correctly. The Cost & ROI Mode remains attic-focused.
Who is behind the site?
Attic Calculator is built and maintained by a small independent team with backgrounds in web engineering and building science estimating. We are not an insulation manufacturer, a Home Depot or Lowe's affiliate, or a contracting company — deliberately. Independence is what lets us publish material data honestly, let you pick any price per bag, and route every disagreement back to the measurement and the code in front of you.
How is the site funded?
Advertising, clearly labeled and kept separate from tool output and editorial content. No affiliate links to material suppliers, no paywall, no premium tier, no data sales — and no behavioral data to sell, because your square footage and climate zone never leave your browser. If an ad ever conflicts with the tool's usefulness, the tool wins.
Is the site really free?
Completely. No sign-up, no usage limits, no watered-down version, no email gate in front of a result. Every calculation runs entirely in your browser — your project numbers never leave your device — and every guide on the site is free to read from top to bottom.
Can the results be used for permit or code compliance?
Treat them as a planning aid attached to your project, not as a code compliance certificate. The calculator's zone targets align with most current energy codes (IECC, IRC, various state codes), but your local authority having jurisdiction may have adopted amendments, and some zones require specific ventilation or air-sealing treatments alongside the insulation R-value. The tool gets you to the right conversation with your contractor or code official; it does not replace their decision.
